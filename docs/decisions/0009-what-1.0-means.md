# 1.0 is a governance commitment, not an evidence threshold

**Status:** accepted. Rewrites the 1.0 criteria.

## Context

The 1.0 criteria as written ask for evidence: the IRI scheme survived a
multi-specification project, the vocabulary went unchanged across two minor
releases, a specification authored by someone else passed the gate. Three of the
five are open, and two of those cannot be closed by writing code.

They also cannot be closed by waiting, which is the part that makes them the
wrong criteria rather than merely unmet. An external author is unlikely to adopt
a specification language that has not reached 1.0, so requiring an external
author before 1.0 is circular. The same holds for a multi-specification project.
The criteria ask for adoption evidence as a precondition for the release that
makes adoption reasonable.

There is one developer and one adopter, and no substantial feedback will arrive
until a user base exists. Any honest plan expects the specification to change
once it does.

## Decision

**1.0 marks a change in who may change the specification, not a claim that the
specification is finished.**

Before 1.0, the author changes the contract unilaterally, on the schedule the
roadmap sets. From 1.0, the author does not change the underlying specification
without consensus among the user base, and changes are raised, discussed, and
ratified rather than decided. That is a real constraint on the author and it is
the thing 1.0 announces.

**2.0 is the designated release for changes arising from that feedback.** The
work already deferred past 1.0 lands there: multi-specification component
identity, and any agent-integration change that touches the graph. Grouping them
is the same batching policy that governed 0.3.0 and 0.11.0, applied to the first
release where the decision is no longer the author's alone.

**The criteria become properties of the artifact and the process,** which are
knowable now, rather than properties of an adoption that has not happened.

## What 1.0 requires instead

1. The graph contract is documented, with a migration path from every prior
   version. *Met: contracts 1 and 2 are published and `specl-migrate` covers
   both the namespace and the contract change.*
2. Every section type, annotation key, and shape is exercised by the corpus.
   *Met and asserted in `tests/test_exit_criteria.py`.*
3. Published IRIs resolve. A stability promise over identifiers that return 404
   is not worth making. *Open: one pull request against perma-id/w3id.org, with
   the delta and procedure in `tools/w3id/README.md`.*
4. Known limitations are documented where an adopter will find them, so that
   what is being accepted is visible rather than discovered. *Open: the
   limitations exist in the roadmap and the contract page; they are not
   collected anywhere an evaluating adopter reads first.*
5. The governance mechanism is written down. A promise not to change something
   unilaterally needs to say who is consulted, how a change is proposed, and
   what happens when there is disagreement. *Open, and the substantive
   remaining work.*

## The mechanism, chosen

The third option: proposals are public and ratified after a fixed comment
period, with silence counting as assent. It works at any community size and
degrades gracefully from zero, which the other two do not. Binding the author
absolutely would hand a veto to whoever happens to be the only adopter;
reserving unilateral action until a threshold makes 1.0 announce something
weaker than it sounds.

`GOVERNANCE.md` is the mechanism. One year, a registry of adopters so that
silence means something, a substantive objection from a registered adopter
blocks rather than being outvoted, and an explicit list of what is *not*
governed so that fixing a defect does not require permission to keep a promise
already made.

The window is a year, and it is shared rather than per proposal: it opens the
day 1.0 ships and closes a year later, and every substantive proposal raised in
it locks to that one date. A shared deadline is something an adopter can put in
a calendar; a rolling one is something nobody can plan around. A year because
specl is aimed at organisations that review their tooling annually, and silence
from someone who never saw the proposal is not assent to anything.

Additive changes are exempt entirely. Gating a change nobody has to migrate for
would make the process an obstacle rather than a protection, and it is the
distinction the whole mechanism rests on.

The cost is that a substantive change waits for the window regardless of how
obviously right it is, and that 2.0 is a year from 1.0 whatever happens.

## Alternatives

**Keep the evidence criteria and delay 1.0.** Rejected as circular: it withholds
the release that makes adoption reasonable until adoption has happened.

**Declare 1.0 with no governance statement.** Rejected. Without a mechanism, "the
author will not change it unilaterally" is an intention, and an adopter cannot
tell an intention from a commitment.

**Skip to 2.0 semantics immediately,** treating the current state as 1.0 and
accepting breaks as they come. Rejected because it discards the only thing 1.0
gives an adopter, which is a period during which the contract does not move.

## Consequences

Criterion 1 as previously written is dropped rather than reworded. It asked for
something a deferred and explicitly breaking piece of work makes unavailable,
and rewording it to say the scheme survived "with limits documented" would have
been an evidence claim dressed to look met.

Criterion 5 is dropped as a gate and kept as a goal. The syntax documentation
being sufficient for an outside author is still the only real test of it, and
the three audits were aimed at that; it is now something 1.0 enables rather than
something it waits on.

`docs/REMAINING.md` and `docs/ROADMAP.md` are rewritten against these criteria.
